[[intro]]
== Introduction

The term _Post-Quantum Cryptography_ (PQC) refers to cryptographic algorithms that have been designed to be secure against attacks with quantum computers. It has been known since the mid-1990s cite:[Shor:1994] that asymptotically efficient quantum algorithms exist against integer factorization and discrete logarithm problems. These problems are central to the security of first-generation public-key cryptography algorithms that were invented in the 1970s and 1980s and came into widespread use as secure Internet protocols and applications were first developed in the 1990s and 2000s.

Since 2016 cite:[NIST:PQC-Call-2016], there has been a focused effort to standardize and adopt newer cryptographic methods that are based on types of mathematical problems for which no efficient quantum attack exists. After a complex evaluation effort, the primary PQC standards were finalized in August 2024.

The main Post-Quantum Cryptography standards published by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) are:

*	*ML-KEM* (FIPS 203, Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism Standard cite:[NIST:FIPS203]). ML-KEM is suitable for asymmetric key establishment and public key encryption.
*	*ML-DSA* (FIPS 204, Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Standard cite:[NIST:FIPS204]). ML-DSA is a signature scheme suitable for asymmetric data and identity authentication.
*	*SLH-DSA* (FIPS 205, Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Standard cite:[NIST:FIPS205]). SLH-DSA is a scheme mostly suitable for static data authentication, although as a stateless scheme, it can also be used for identity authentication.

In the profiled implementations, Keccak-p[1600,24] often accounts for more
than 50% of ML-KEM and ML-DSA execution time. A compiled 24-round permutation
uses over 4,000 instructions even when the scalar `rori` and `andn` instructions
are available, explaining why SHAKE expansion becomes a bottleneck once other
parts of the algorithms are vectorized. Preliminary benchmarks of `vkeccak.vi`
on a VLEN=256 proof-of-concept implementation report average end-to-end speedups
of 1.45x for ML-KEM and 1.59x for ML-DSA when the instruction is added to
implementations already using vector intrinsics. See
link:https://karucore.com/posts/pqc-and-keccak-on-karu/[PQC and Keccak on Karu]
for the benchmark methodology and detailed results.

NIST selected HQC as a second PQC KEM cite:[NIST:IR8545] and is developing it
as FIPS 207 cite:[NIST:FIPS207-Status]. FN-DSA, based on Falcon, is being
developed as FIPS 206 cite:[NIST:FIPS206-Status]. The Keccak instructions
proposed here also accelerate the SHA-3 and SHAKE components of HQC and
Falcon/FN-DSA, as well as the SHAKE256 parameter sets of the LMS and XMSS
stateful hash-based signature schemes specified in SP 800-208
cite:[NIST:SP800-208].

[NOTE]
====
Before the transition to quantum-safe cryptography, the tasks of ML-KEM were usually accomplished with (Elliptic Curve) Diffie-Hellman (DH/ECDH) Key Establishment cite:[IETF:RFC7748,NIST:SP800-56Ar3] or with RSA Public-Key Encryption cite:[IETF:RFC8017,NIST:SP800-56Br2]. The tasks of ML-DSA and SLH-DSA signature schemes were usually accomplished with either RSA Signatures cite:[IETF:RFC8017] or with ECDSA/EdDSA Elliptic Curve signatures cite:[IETF:RFC8032,NIST:FIPS186-5].

During the transition period, many applications will be using hybrids where both classical and their PQC counterpart algorithms are executed.
====

ML-KEM already sees widespread deployment in TLS 1.3 as part of the
X25519MLKEM768 hybrid key-agreement group. RFC 10024 defines this group and
identifies it as often the most practical choice for a single PQ/T hybrid
combiner cite:[IETF:RFC10024].
